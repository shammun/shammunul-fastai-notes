DDPM, Step by Step: A Complete Mathematical Derivation

Every equation from the original Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models paper, built up from first principles — no math swept under the rug.

Why diffusion?

Diffusion models took over image generation almost overnight. They power Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2, Midjourney, Imagen — anything that hands you a photorealistic picture of an astronaut riding a horse. But the original paper, Ho et al. (2020), is a wall of probability theory. Most explanations skip the hard equations. This post does the opposite: we derive every equation from scratch, define every symbol the first time it appears, and check our work with PyTorch code at the end.

The basic idea is silly enough to fit on a napkin. Take a clean image. Add a little Gaussian noise. Add a little more. Keep going for 1,000 steps until the image is pure television static. Then train a neural network to undo one step of that process. If the network learns to denoise one step at a time, you can chain 1,000 of its predictions to generate a brand-new image starting from pure noise.

Everything below is the math that makes "add noise, learn to remove it" actually work — the loss function, the closed-form formulas that skip the 1,000-step chain during training, and the sampling algorithm.

Audience floor. You know what a function and a probability are. You don't need to know what a Gaussian is, what conditional probability is, what Bayes' rule says, or what PyTorch does. I'll define each on first use.

The forward process — adding noise step by step

We start with a real image. Call it x₀ — the subscript 0 means "step zero, no noise added yet." We're going to corrupt it by adding a little Gaussian noise at each of $T$ time steps, producing a sequence:

x₀ → x₁ → x₂ → … → x_T

"Gaussian noise" just means a random number drawn from a bell curve centered at 0. Writing ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) means "ε is a noise vector where every coordinate is independently drawn from a standard bell curve." The symbol 𝒩 is the Gaussian (or "normal") distribution, the 0 is its mean, and I is the identity matrix — fancy way of saying "every pixel gets its own independent noise."

The one-step forward process is defined as:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\; \beta_t \mathbf{I}\big)$$

Reading this: given the previous image $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, the next image $\mathbf{x}_t$ is drawn from a Gaussian whose mean is the previous image shrunk slightly toward zero (multiplied by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$), and whose variance is $\beta_t$. The schedule $\beta_t$ is tiny at the start (around 0.0001) and grows slowly toward 0.02 at the end, so each individual step adds only a whisper of noise.

Equivalently — and this is how we'll usually write it — we sample $\mathbf{x}_t$ as:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$
Why the square roots? Because variances add. The forward step is engineered so that if $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ has unit variance, then $\mathbf{x}_t$ also has unit variance: $(1-\beta_t) + \beta_t = 1$. Without the $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ shrinkage, the variance would blow up over 1,000 steps. With it, after $T$ steps we land cleanly at $\mathbf{x}_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ — pure static.

Skipping ahead — the closed-form jump

If we want $\mathbf{x}_5$ from $\mathbf{x}_0$, do we really have to run the chain five times? No — because of a beautiful property of Gaussians. Define:

$$\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, \qquad \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$$

$\alpha_t$ is "how much of the signal we keep in one step" (close to 1). $\bar{\alpha}_t$ is "how much of the original image survives after $t$ steps" — starting at 1 and dropping toward 0 as $t \to T$. The bar over $\alpha$ just signals it's a cumulative quantity.

The remarkable fact is that we can jump straight from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to any $\mathbf{x}_t$ in one shot:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

Why this works. Expand two steps:

$$\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}\,\mathbf{x}_1 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_2}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}\,(\sqrt{\alpha_1}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_1}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_2}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2$$

The two noise terms $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2$ are independent Gaussians. When you add independent Gaussians, variances add. So $\sqrt{\alpha_2(1-\alpha_1)}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_2}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2$ is just another Gaussian with variance $\alpha_2(1-\alpha_1) + (1-\alpha_2) = 1 - \alpha_1\alpha_2 = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_2$. Replace the sum with a single scaled $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ and you get $\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. Induction on $t$ gives the boxed formula.

This single fact is what makes DDPM training fast. We never simulate the 1,000-step chain — we just pick a random $t$, jump straight from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to $\mathbf{x}_t$, and ask the network to remove the noise.

The reverse process — where the neural network enters

If we can corrupt an image with $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})$, can we run that backward? Given a noisy $\mathbf{x}_t$, can we recover $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$? In principle yes — Bayes' rule gives us the exact reverse distribution. But that exact reverse depends on the full data distribution of all possible images, which we don't have a formula for.

The trick: approximate the true reverse distribution with a neural network. We model the reverse step as another Gaussian and let a network predict its mean. Call the network's parameters $\theta$:

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t),\; \Sigma_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\big)$$

The network sees the noisy image $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the current timestep $t$, and outputs the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ of where the slightly-less-noisy $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ should live. The variance $\Sigma_\theta$ is usually fixed to a constant in the simple version.

Now we have two problems. What should the network actually predict? And what loss function trains it?

The ELBO — a loss function we can compute

We'd love to maximize $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ — the log-probability our model assigns to a real image. But computing that requires integrating over every possible noise trajectory $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ that could have produced $\mathbf{x}_0$. That's an integral over a million-dimensional space. Impossible.

Variational inference gives us a workaround: a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ that is computable, called the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO). After algebra you can find in any VAE textbook, the negative ELBO for diffusion decomposes into three friendly pieces:

$$L = \underbrace{D_\mathrm{KL}\!\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \,\Vert\, p(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{L_T} \;+\; \sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\!\big[D_\mathrm{KL}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0) \,\Vert\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t))\big]}_{L_{t-1}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)]}_{L_0}$$

Three pieces. Each one tells you something concrete:

  • $L_T$ measures how close $\mathbf{x}_T$ (the noisiest image) is to a standard Gaussian. We designed the forward schedule so this is already true; $L_T$ has no learnable parameters and is essentially a constant.
  • $L_{t-1}$ (the meat of the loss) measures, at every timestep $t$, how close our network's reverse distribution $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ is to the "true" reverse $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ that knows the answer $\mathbf{x}_0$. Match these and we win.
  • $L_0$ is a tiny boundary term — the network's likelihood of reconstructing the clean image from $\mathbf{x}_1$. In practice it gets folded into the same $L_{t-1}$ formula.
What is KL divergence? $D_\mathrm{KL}(P \,\Vert\, Q)$ is the average "surprise" you'd feel if you expected $Q$ but the world was actually $P$. It's zero when $P=Q$ and positive otherwise. For two Gaussians it has a closed form — see below.

Plain English version: at every timestep, the network's guess for the reverse step must match the true reverse step. Train it on that and you have a denoising diffusion model.

The true posterior $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$

What's that "true reverse" $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ that the network is chasing? It's the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ given that we already saw $\mathbf{x}_t$ and we know the clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$. Apply Bayes' rule:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \;=\; \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_0)\,q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}$$

All three pieces on the right are Gaussians whose formulas we already wrote down. So the left side is a product/quotient of Gaussians — which is itself a Gaussian. After collecting exponents and completing the square (it's tedious algebra, but no new ideas), you land at:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\; \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0),\; \tilde{\beta}_t \mathbf{I}\big)$$ $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 \;+\; \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_t$$ $$\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\beta_t$$

This is the target our network is trying to match. The mean is a weighted blend of the clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$ and the current noisy $\mathbf{x}_t$. The weights are deterministic functions of the noise schedule. The variance $\tilde{\beta}_t$ is also fully determined by the schedule — no neural network needed.

KL between two Gaussians — a closed-form per-step loss

The middle term $L_{t-1}$ is a KL divergence between two Gaussians. For two Gaussians with the same variance $\Sigma$ but different means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \boldsymbol{\mu}_2$:

$$D_\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_2, \Sigma)\big) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\Vert \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \Vert^2$$

(For isotropic $\Sigma = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$.) So the loss at timestep $t$ is just the squared distance between the network's predicted mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ and the true posterior mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$, divided by some constant:

$$L_{t-1} \;\propto\; \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\Vert \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\Vert^2\right]$$

Mean squared error between two means. Almost simple enough to be disappointing.

The big simplification — predict the noise, not the mean

One last trick. Remember the closed-form forward jump:

$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$

Solving for $\mathbf{x}_0$:

$\mathbf{x}_0 = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)$

If we substitute this expression for $\mathbf{x}_0$ into the formula for $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$ and simplify, the cancellations are heroic. After dust settles:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)$$

The true posterior mean depends only on $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ that was used to create $\mathbf{x}_t$. So instead of asking the network to predict the mean directly, we ask it to predict the noise. Define the noise-prediction network $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ and parameterize:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right)$$

Now the MSE loss simplifies further. The network predicts $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$; the target is the actual $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ we used in the forward step. The training objective collapses to:

$$L_\text{simple}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\Vert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\; t) \Vert^2\right]$$

This is the actual loss used in the DDPM paper. Pick a real image, pick a random timestep $t$, pick random noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, noise the image, ask the network to predict the noise back. Mean squared error. That's the whole training objective.

This is the punchline. A thousand pages of variational inference collapse to "add noise, predict the noise, use MSE." The math wasn't decorative — every step we did was necessary to confirm that this absurdly simple loss is a valid lower bound on the actual likelihood.

The noise schedule — what $\beta_t$ should look like

We've been writing $\beta_t$ symbolically. What numbers do we actually plug in? The original DDPM paper uses a linear schedule: $\beta_t$ rises linearly from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$ over $T = 1000$ steps. Later work (Nichol & Dhariwal 2021) found a cosine schedule destroys signal more gradually and trains better, especially at lower resolutions.

Linear vs cosine schedule

Two views: $\beta_t$ (the per-step noise variance) and $\bar{\alpha}_t$ (the fraction of original signal surviving after $t$ steps). The cosine schedule keeps more signal in the middle of the chain — useful for training stability.

Sampling — from pure noise back to an image

Training is half the game. At inference time, we want to generate a new image. Start from $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ — actual TV static. Then for $t = T, T-1, \ldots, 1$, sample:

$$\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right) + \sigma_t \mathbf{z}, \quad \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$$

The first term is the predicted mean — exactly the $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ we parameterized in §7. The added $\sigma_t \mathbf{z}$ is a small kick of fresh noise, so we're not collapsing the distribution. Typical choice is $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta_t}$ (the original paper) or $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_t}$ (the posterior variance, slightly different).

The code, distilled

Here's the entire DDPM training and sampling loop in ~30 lines of PyTorch. This is pulled from 15_DDPM_explained.ipynb in the fast.ai notes — the same noise schedule, the same noisify, the same sampling step we derived above.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# ----- Noise schedule (§8) -----
def linear_schedule(n_steps, beta_min=1e-4, beta_max=0.02):
    beta      = torch.linspace(beta_min, beta_max, n_steps)
    alpha     = 1. - beta
    alphabar  = alpha.cumprod(dim=0)
    sigma     = beta.sqrt()
    return beta, alpha, alphabar, sigma

# ----- Forward: closed-form jump x_0 -> x_t (§2) -----
def noisify(x0, alphabar):
    t       = torch.randint(0, len(alphabar), (x0.shape[0],), device=x0.device)
    eps     = torch.randn_like(x0)
    ab_t    = alphabar[t].reshape(-1, 1, 1, 1)   # broadcast over CHW
    xt      = ab_t.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab_t).sqrt() * eps
    return (xt, t), eps                          # network input, target

# ----- Training loss: predict the noise (§7) -----
def ddpm_loss(model, x0, alphabar):
    (xt, t), eps = noisify(x0, alphabar)
    eps_pred     = model(xt, t)                  # epsilon_theta(x_t, t)
    return F.mse_loss(eps_pred, eps)             # L_simple

# ----- Sampling: reverse the chain (§9) -----
@torch.no_grad()
def sample(model, shape, beta, alpha, alphabar, sigma):
    x = torch.randn(shape, device=alpha.device)  # start from pure noise
    for t in reversed(range(len(beta))):
        z       = torch.randn_like(x) if t > 0 else 0
        eps_hat = model(x, torch.full((shape[0],), t, device=x.device))
        x       = (x - beta[t]/(1-alphabar[t]).sqrt() * eps_hat) / alpha[t].sqrt()
        x       = x + sigma[t] * z
    return x

The model is typically a U-Net that takes a noisy image and a timestep, and outputs a noise tensor of the same shape. That's the only learnable component. Everything else is the math we just derived.

What we covered

  • Forward process: noise the image one tiny Gaussian step at a time. A clever choice of coefficients keeps variance at 1 throughout the chain.
  • Closed-form jump: independent Gaussians add nicely. We can skip from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to any $\mathbf{x}_t$ in one shot: $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.
  • Reverse process: a neural network parameterizes $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$ as a Gaussian.
  • ELBO: a tractable lower bound on $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ that decomposes into KL terms — one per timestep — each comparing the network's reverse step to the true posterior.
  • True posterior: Bayes' rule + completing the square gives $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t, \tilde{\beta}_t \mathbf{I})$ — a closed-form target.
  • Loss: KL between two Gaussians of equal variance is just MSE between means.
  • Simplification: reparameterize so the network predicts the noise instead of the mean. The loss collapses to $\Vert\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\Vert^2$.
  • Sampling: start from Gaussian noise, run the reverse step 1,000 times, get an image.

DDPM is one of those rare papers where the math, once derived, feels inevitable. Every step justifies the next, and the final training objective is almost laughably simple. Subsequent work (DDIM, classifier-free guidance, latent diffusion, score-based generative models) builds directly on this foundation.
